# STAR story bank — Customer Success Manager

Six stories covering all four themes in `jd-analysis.md`. Reuse them across questions —
most CSM interview questions are one of these four themes in disguise. Do not invent new
stories under pressure.

**Everything factual here comes from the LinkedIn profile. The Results are
`[[NEED: ...]]` because only Vanshika knows them — fill them in before any interview. A
story without a Result is an anecdote, and interviewers notice.**

---

## Story 1 — "I built the playbook that did not exist"
**Maps to:** Theme 3 (onboarding & enablement) — *the strongest story in the bank; lead with it*
**Use for:** "Tell me about yourself" · "Biggest accomplishment" · "How do you approach onboarding?"

**Situation.** I joined saasguru as a founding team member. It was a SaaS company selling
certification training, operating in Australia, the US and the UK, and there was no
operational documentation of any kind — every customer got a slightly different experience
depending on who happened to handle them.

**Task.** I owned operations and training. Making the inconsistency go away was mine to
solve, across three regions and three time zones, with no existing template to copy.

**Action.** I wrote the SOPs and quality checklists from scratch, one process at a time,
starting with the ones customers complained about most. I built onboarding bootcamps so
new customers hit their first milestone in a structured way rather than by luck. I set up
a townhall cadence so feedback had somewhere to go. And I put a standing loop in with the
product team, so recurring friction became roadmap input instead of a thing operations
absorbed quietly every week.

**Result.** `[[NEED: NPS before → after]]`. The SOPs became the operating standard across
all three regions and outlasted my time there — `[[NEED: any figure on consistency,
handling time, or onboarding duration]]`.

**If pushed — "how did you decide what to write first?"** I ranked by frequency times pain.
Anything that generated repeat contacts got documented first, because that is where
inconsistency compounds. I did not try to document everything; I documented what was
actually breaking.

## Story 2 — "The customer whose calendar could not move"
**Maps to:** Theme 1 (proactive ownership) + escalation management
**Use for:** "Difficult customer" · "Escalation you handled" · "Time you spotted a problem early"

**Situation.** At Superset our customers run campus hiring cycles. Placement season dates
are fixed by the academic calendar — if a customer is not live on time, they do not
reschedule, they lose the season.

**Task.** `[[NEED: pick one specific account and one specific near-miss — a delayed
configuration, an integration that broke mid-drive, a stakeholder change during onboarding.
This story is much stronger with a real named situation.]]`

**Action.** `[[NEED: what you specifically did — first person. Who you escalated to
internally, what you changed on the customer side, how you communicated while it was
unresolved.]]`

**Result.** `[[NEED: outcome — did they go live on time, did they renew, what did the
relationship look like afterwards?]]`

**If pushed — "what would you do differently?"** Have a good answer ready. The honest one is
usually about detecting it earlier, and that answer sets up your view on health scoring.

## Story 3 — "Five hundred relationships, one at a time"
**Maps to:** Theme 1 (relationship ownership at scale) + Theme 2 (retention)
**Use for:** "How do you prioritise a large book?" · "How do you scale personal attention?"

**Situation.** At WhiteHat Jr I held over 500 paid international customer relationships
across the US, UK and Gulf markets — parents who had bought a course for their child and
whose satisfaction determined whether they continued.

**Task.** Own satisfaction and continuation across the whole book. At that volume you
cannot treat every account identically, so the real task was deciding where attention went.

**Action.** I segmented by risk rather than by revenue — engagement signals, contact
history, how a customer's last interaction had ended. I front-loaded proactive outreach to
the at-risk segment instead of waiting for complaints, on the principle that the customers
about to leave are usually the ones who have stopped complaining. For the healthy segment I
kept contact light and predictable.

**Result.** `[[NEED: retention or continuation rate across the book]]`.

**If pushed — "what signal predicted churn best?"** Answer honestly from experience.
Silence after an unresolved issue is the usual one, and it is a good answer because it
shows you were reading behaviour, not dashboards.

## Story 4 — "Getting product to care"
**Maps to:** Theme 4 (cross-functional influence)
**Use for:** "How do you work with product?" · "Influencing without authority" · "Time you disagreed with another team"

**Situation.** At saasguru the same customer friction kept surfacing in operations every
week, and operations kept absorbing it manually. Product had its own roadmap and no
particular reason to prioritise our complaints.

**Task.** Get the recurring problems in front of product in a form they could act on,
without any authority over their roadmap.

**Action.** I stopped forwarding individual complaints and started bringing patterns — how
often something occurred, how much operational time it cost, which customers it touched. I
built a standing coordination rhythm with the product team rather than escalating ad hoc,
so the conversation was continuous instead of adversarial. I framed each issue in terms of
what it cost the business, not what it cost my team.

**Result.** `[[NEED: a specific change that shipped because you raised it — this is the
single most valuable detail to add to this bank]]`.

**If pushed — "what if product still says no?"** The good answer: you accept the
prioritisation, you tell the customer the truth about timelines, and you build the manual
workaround without pretending it is a fix.

## Story 5 — "Operating across three time zones"
**Maps to:** Theme 2 (KPIs) + organisational strength
**Use for:** "Time management" · "Competing priorities" · "Working with distributed teams"

**Situation.** At Multiplier I ran client-facing operations across APAC — a global
employment platform where customer issues frequently crossed legal, payroll and product,
and no single team owned the resolution.

**Task.** Resolve complex client queries end to end against service KPIs, in a region
spanning enough time zones that most issues could not be closed in a single working day.

**Action.** I owned issues rather than routing them — I stayed the customer's single point
of contact even when the resolution sat in three other teams, and I chased internally
rather than making the customer chase. I restructured the recurring workflows so handoffs
happened at predictable points instead of wherever the ticket happened to stall.

**Result.** Consistently surpassed service KPIs; `[[NEED: CSAT movement, or resolution time
before → after]]`.

## Story 6 — "Negotiating when both sides need the deal"
**Maps to:** Theme 4 (negotiation, complex accounts)
**Use for:** "Negotiation experience" · "Commercial conversation" · covers the JD's negotiation requirement

**Situation.** At JSM I programmed live music across every Hard Rock Café outlet in India
and had to make the economics work — 35 to 40 artists a week, against venue revenue targets.

**Task.** Negotiate sponsor agreements and vendor contracts that met venue targets without
degrading the guest experience the brand depended on.

**Action.** I negotiated sponsorship terms directly, structured distribution partnerships
with BookMyShow, Insider and Skillbox to widen reach rather than discount, and worked
vendor contracts down where it did not touch quality.

**Result.** `[[NEED: revenue, attendance, or cost figure]]`.

**Framing note.** Use this only when asked directly about negotiation or commercial
experience. Do not volunteer the events background otherwise — it dilutes the SaaS
narrative. When you do use it, land it in one sentence: "before I moved into SaaS I ran
event programming commercially, which is where I learned to negotiate."

## Delivery rules

- **90 seconds spoken.** Time yourself once per story. Action is the longest section.
- **Say "I", not "we".** "We" hides your contribution and interviewers are trained to hear it.
- **Result needs a number.** Every `[[NEED]]` above is a story that is not yet ready.
- **Have one failure story.** You will be asked. Story 2's "what would you do differently"
  can carry it, but a dedicated one is better.
